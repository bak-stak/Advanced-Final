Populist parties: still a home for the politically distrustful, or a faded and institutionalized phenomenon?

Author

Giovanni Bacchiega

Published

June 20, 2026

Research question and hypothesis

Italian populist parties rise and fall

In the 2018 Italian political elections 5 star movement and League for Salvini premier were the biggest winners obtaining respectively 32% and 17% of the votes; these two can be considered the main populist Italian parties . The dissatisfaction toward political parties and their system was one of the main driver for their rise (Chiaramonte et al., 2018) .

4 years later these 2 parties fell: 5 star movement obtained about 15,43% but the worst fall was the League one, collapsing to 8.8%.

Younger generations and democracy

Lots of studies in recent years showed a growing disaffection and distrust of younger generations toward democracy and its institutions and parties (e.g. Kwak et al., 2020). This phenomenon frequently lead to two different attitudes that influence their behavior when they are called to the polls: democratic apathy and democratic antipathy; the former leading to abstention, the latter to vote for anti-system/populist parties (Foa & Mounk, 2019).

Research questions and hypothesis

The purpose of the present study is to check the grade with which these two parties are still able to answer the distrustful and protesting political supply and whether it changes for age cohorts in the 2022 Italian general elections.

The main research question will be the following:

  • How much the distrust toward political and party system translates in voting for populist parties? How does It changes for different cohorts?

It will be articulated in 2 main hypothesis:

H1: Higher level of distrust toward Parliament and political parties will tend to significantly increase the share of votes for populist parties.

H2: The positive effect of institutional distrust on populist voting will be weaker among younger cohorts (18-34), who are more likely to convert distrust into abstention rather than partisan choice.

Data and variable operationalization

Data source

To operationalize distrust and its relation with voting behavior the ITANES post-electoral data from 2022 elections (Vezzoni et al., 2023) has been employed; it provides individual level observations for 4696 individuals.

Selected variables and operationalization

Institutional distrust: To operationalize this dimension an index was built. To do so factor analysis and principal component analysis were performed on 5 selected items. The analyses highlighted 2 main dimensions1: institutional distrust and stealth democracy2, both theoretically correlated with populism. Nevertheless only the first dimension was employed for the present study as it was better defined and, theoretically, a predictor for both populism and abstention.

Institutional distrust is composed by three items: trust toward parliament, government and parties. This three items had range 1-4 were 1 meant a lot of trust. I then constructed a weighted index and rescaled it 0-10 for interpretability.

Populist vote: votes for 5SM and League were coded as populist and other as non populist. The selection of this two come from CHES & POPPA (Rovny et al., 2025; Zaslove et al., 2024) scores, two of the main expert surveys about political parties3 .

Abstention: for abstention the voted/non voted variable from ITANES was employed

Age class: the survey presented a 6 class age classification which were recoded to 5 for 2 reasons:

  1. The main hypothesis is about younger generations which include also 25-34. The study main model will be a logistic model, which require a reference category; having them both as reference category will help to compare them directly with other cohorts;
  2. 19-24 and 24-35 are the two less numerous categories, specially the lower one which presented less than a half observation respect to the others. Merging them was useful to have stronger predictive power.

Macro Region: for electoral behavior geographical positioning is always a strong predictor in Italy, observations were divided them in the 3 classical macro regions: north, south and red zone (Toscana, Emilia-Romagna, Marche, Umbria).

Model choice and statistical justification

Main model

To test H1 a logistic regression model has been employed for both populist vote and abstention. This kind of regression is appropriate as it models the outcome as a probability within the [0,1] range and both the dependent variables are binary. Main independent variable was institutional distrust.

The initial univariate analysis has been performed and it led to a small effect, suggesting potential misspecification of the model, particularly the violation of the linearity assumption in the logit.

Since the logistic regression failed to produce strong significative results a Generalized addittive model (GAM) was employed as a diagnostic tool to check if the fitting problem was due to data distribution; it was ideal because it doesn’t assume linearity and adapt to the shape of the data and reveal potential non linear patterns. This showed a non-monotonic relationship between distrust and populist voting with a collapse in populist voting for high levels of distrust, and, on the contrary, abstention displays a consistently positive relationship.

Based on this evidence a restricted specification, labelled Critical Distrust Threshold (CDT), was introduced. It restricted the sample respondents below the threshold of 8. A better fitting on this data would confirm that distrust works as a positive predictor till medium-high levels of distrust.

Interaction and Additive model

To test H2 age class was added and incorporated in both additive and interaction models. The aim of the first one was to study the effect of age class isolated; the second to check whether distrust interacted with age class, trying to have a better comprehension of how distrust translates to electoral behavior for each age class. The selected reference category has been 18-34, as it was the main focus of the study: this choice permitted to compare it to all the other classes.

Lastly a control for Macro Region was included, to account for persistent territorial differences in voting behavior.

Results

Institutional distrust on populist vote and abstention

The first univariate model aimed to capture the effect of distrust on populism and abstention. Both resulted significant but the coefficient for abstention was substantially larger. The coefficients for logistic regression are presented in log-odds, a 1 point increase in distrust would increase the log-odds of voting populist of 0.05, though this is a scale that lacks intuitive interpretability. A common approximation is to use the “divide by 4 rule” dividing the parameter by 4 to have the slope of the curve in its steepest part around 0.5 probability, however, since the predicted probabilities lies below that threshold the marginal effect would be overestimated.

To interpet these coefficients two operations were performed: (1) exponentiating the logistic coefficient to obtain an odds ratio; (2) computing the derivative at the average value of institutional distrust in ITANES data to evaluate the predictive difference.

  • Populist vote: the exponentiation lead to an odds ratio of 1.05, this indicate that a unit increase in institutional distrust increases the odds of voting populist of 5.1%. Moreover at the mean distrust level (6.2) the probability of voting populist is 18.3% with a marginal effect at the mean of 0.008. From the graphical representation below it can be appreciated the approximately constant marginal effect of distrust on populist vote.
  • Abstention: as can be appreciated immediately from the graph comparison the effect of distrust on abstention is way stronger, starting from below 5% for distrust = 0, but with increasing marginal effects. The slope at the mean distrust level is 0.026, meaning that a 1 point increment at 6.2 would increment the probability of abstaining 2.6%

CDT model

The results of the prior analysis highlighted a weak coefficient and lower significativeness for populism. To better understand the reason a general addictive model was performed as an explorative model; this has the advantage of not assessing a linear shape and fits the best possible value of y for each value of x.

The populist GAM model clearly presents a point in which the relationship between populist vote and distrust reverse its direction in a critical threshold, while the same effect doesn’t happen for abstention. GAM centers values around the mean, the values on the y axis are in log-odds. Observation below the 0 have therefore minor probabilities of voting populist/abstaining than the average level of the sample. The lines on the graph floor represent the distribution of the observations.

To check whether the logistic model is well performing till that threshold a new model with observation which presented a level of distrust lower then eight (the critical threshold) was computed and labelled as Critical Distrust Threshold (CDT) model. The changes in the data were substantial as 987 observation presented a high level (>8) of distrust.

CDT model showed stronger results in significativity and intensity of the effect. P-value, the main measure for significativity, decreased below 0.001, and log-odds were more than doubled. This is reflected across all interpretative metrics: odd ratio increases from 1.05 to 1.14, the probability of voting populist at the sample mean goes from 18.3 to 19.6. Most importantly the marginal effect at the mean increases of 0.013, that means that an increase of 1 point in distrust at that level raises the probability of voting populist by 2.1 percentage points (against the previous 0.8%). These stronger estimates are more consistent with the GAM findings: including high distrust voters in the full model attenuates the positive association observed at lower distrust levels.

Age model: additive and interaction

Age classes were then added to the model both through additive and interactions models. The different specifications aim is to compute: (1) for additive model the specific mere effect of age class on populist voting and abstention, (2) for interaction models how each age class act as a moderator for the effect that institutional distrust has on the two dependent variables. The selected reference class was 18-34.

The regression table yields few significant results overall. Institutional distrust, however, remains a significant predictor across all specifications, and most strongly in the CDT models, in line with previous discover. Nevertheless it is worth noting some exceptions:

  • Age 35-44: there is significativeness for additive model, indicating that there is a somewhat higher populist tendency, but the relation between distrust and populist vote remain quite stable.

  • Age 45-54: this cohort present the most interesting pattern: coefficients get significant once the interaction term is introduced in the computation and it has an opposite sign in the two occurrences. The most plausible explanation for this result is an higher general tendency of this age class to vote for populist parties that, on the contrary, has less to do with distrust compared to the reference class. The CDT interaction plot below illustrates this. Interaction was crucial to capture this tendency, as without it the two effects nullified each other.

  • Age 55+: Neither cohort reaches significance in any specification.

The interaction plot confirms the pattern: there is a different behavior of class 45-54, that even present a slightly negative relationship between institutional distrust and populist vote. It is also worth noting that younger generations and 64+ present a really similar attitude toward populist parties, more of that are discussed in the interpretation section.

The models yield some interesting results, with a general pattern that emerges far more sharply in the interaction specifications. The negative coefficient observed in the additive models turns positive once the interaction is introduced: this indicates that the greater tendency of the young to abstain is a general, baseline disposition — one that is less sensitive to distrust — whereas for the other cohorts the effect of distrust is significantly more positive. The only comparable case is that of the over-64 cohort, for whom abstention does not appear to be a function of distrust; this point, too, is developed further in the interpretation section.

The plot reveals these tendencies as the lower cohort curve is the flattest one while the others follows similar paths, despite the 65+ cohort that also assumes a flatter shape.

Region controlling

Lastly to assess the robustness of the findings a control for region was performed.

For what pertains populism attitudes the patterns remain the same seen in the previous section with lower significativity; south area emerge to be the strongest predictor for populist vote as widely recognized in literature, specially in the support for 5SM.

On the contrary abstention isn’t related to geographical location and the models remain exactly the same. Also this last finding is coherent with literature which confirm that in 2022 elections abstention was a wide-spread phenomenon through all the peninsula.

Interpretation and substantive insights

The objective of this paper was to study how distrust translated to vote for populist parties and abstension, with a particular focus on the different age cohorts, specially the younger ones.

The first hypothesis, about the positive relation between distrust and populist vote in the 2022 Italian election is confirmed for medium-high levels of distrust, even if the distrust coefficient on abstention (0.199 in log-odds) is more than three times larger than its counterparts for populist voting (0.062) . But evidences also demonstred that, there is a threshold (~8 on distrust index), which has been labelled Critical Distrust Threshold, discovered with a GAM model, from which the support for populist parties collapses, in favor of abstention. Moreover abstenstion showed stronger correlation with distrust trhough all the fitted models; meaning that the main answers to this sentiment is leaving the ballots. Probably the discontent that once flowed to the Five Star Movement and the league moved to the abstention party. Nevertheless the link between populist parties and distrust remain, and could also be absorbed by Brothers of Italy (this will be discussed in the successive section).

The second part of the study pertained the interaction between age, populism/abstention and institutional distrust. The insights were various.

middle age cohorts tend to be more inclined to vote for populist parties, nevertheless this happens with different patterns: 35-44 and 55-64 presented significative higher levels of populism in additive model through all the sample, which decreased or even vanished in interaction model. This suggests a major tendency which has few to do with distrust. The most peculiar pattern is the 45-54 age cohort which presented a positive coefficient in additive models but a strong negative one in Interaction ones, indicating a general tendency to vote for populist parties which is somewhat anelastic to distrust. These findings suggest the presence of a “populist generation”, the 45-54 one, which can also be extended to the 35-64 for the general tendency to prefer populism irrespective of distrust. These are the generation which were mostly impacted by the crisis that followed one another since 2008 and assisted to emerging of 5SM but also to the league’s one as a national party (Algan et al., 2017).

Regarding the youngest and oldest classes their general behavior seems to be pretty similar: the interecept is lower but the slope get steeper as distrust goes above 6. but the underlying cause could be really different: for younger generations this could be due to a lower closeness to populist parties which emerged when they were too young, and their is more oriented to abstention, while the older generation is probably more bound to classical parties and protest vote for populist parties is correlated only with strong distrustful positions.

These effects are well explained from the marginal effect plot which shows the different average slope for age classes. Even though interesting this is also a warning to be cautious toward these findings as confidence intervals are mostly overlapping.

For what pertain abstention, the hypothesis about younger classes is somewhat inexact: abstention is not the answer of younger generations to distrust but their general attitude with a constant slope. On the contrary middle age classes are the ones which present the higher marginal effects when the distrust reach medium high level, while younger ones present sharply higher levels of abstention for lower distrust scenarios. Eventually there is the 65+ class for which abstention is hardly an option, this is a class more socialized to politics, which lived the mass parties of the first republic, such behavior is coherent with literature.

Limitations and reflection

Several limitations qualify this study specially on the theoretical level. The main concerns regard the operationalisation of populism for two reasons:

  1. Placing brothers of italy in the non-populist pool might be an hazard; even if league and 5SM are the 2 parties that are classically considered as populist; in recent years, for a lot of scholars, Brothers of Italy joined the two as a typical example of far-right nationalist populism. Moreover one of its main claims was being the only opposition party who opposed to the party system; a typical populist frame.
  2. Attitudes toward 5SM and League could be very different, specifically macro-region wise, the former having it’s base in southern Italy, the latter having its roots in the north. Nevertheless this could also be a positive factor, having the Matteo Salvini’s party capturing distrust and dissatifaction in the north, and the Giuseppe Conte’s one doing the same in the south. This requires deeper analyses.

Moreover the models control for macro-regions but don’t do the same for classic predictors such as education, occupational status, and income; which are also strong predictors for 5SM.

The interaction models which provide some of the main insights are still very fragile, interval confidence are pretty wide; the findings of the present must be taken with caution and studied carefully and not be taken as established. Also the CDT specification, since it is data-driven, must be studied in deep even if the cutoff in the GAM seems to be pretty relevant.

Finally, to give a proper answer to whether populism is fading and becoming institutionalized, with more and more electors leaving the ballots instead of voting populist vote as an answer to distrust, a comparing with the 2018 elections would be required. Evidences in the present reveal that distrust is a stronger way to abstention than populist vote, and electoral flows, populist parties electoral downfall and rise of abstention seems to confirm the trend.

Further studies could add panel studies to compute the evolution of attitudes toward populism, and even cross-national elements as populism is a wide-spread phenomenon in most European countries.
